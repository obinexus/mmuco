MMUCO Orion OS — Hive System and Ecological Food Production Brief
(Brief as supplied by the user, 25 Oct 2026; copied here unchanged as the source for this workspace.)

1. Core design clarification
The existing MMUCO Orion Duplex must incorporate a Hive System: an open, productive garden designed to support the ecosystem and sustainably produce food for residents.
The Hive is not merely decorative landscaping, a conventional vegetable patch, or an enclosed greenhouse. It is a proposed ecological food-production system that connects the dwelling to the natural environment.
The Hive concept belongs within the wider MMUCO Orion OS vision: integrating food, water, shelter and basic energy into a sustainable living environment.
Preserve the distinction between:

1. The Orion Duplex housing node.
2. The duplex's associated Hive garden and food-production system.
3. Shared compound facilities, including any public garden, patio, pathways and community amenities.

Do not merge these into one undifferentiated space. Record the exact ownership, access and boundaries of each area as design decisions requiring confirmation.
2. The Hive garden
Design a provisional, open-air ecological garden around a coordinated food-production model.
The proposed system includes:

* Crop-growing areas: designated plots for crops such as beans, rice, yam and potatoes, subject to climate, soil, water availability and local growing conditions.
* Fish-and-rice garden: investigate a possible integrated rice-and-fish growing system, sometimes called rice–fish co-culture. Keep this as a proposed subsystem until water depth, water quality, drainage, fish species, crop requirements and local suitability have been assessed.
* Water infrastructure: explore rainwater harvesting from the roof, storage, filtration and irrigation. Any well, pond or other water source must be represented separately from treated drinking-water infrastructure.
* Pollinator habitat: provide suitable flowering plants, nesting habitat and pesticide-conscious management to support bees and other beneficial pollinators.
* Biodiversity: allow for native plants, soil organisms and other useful ecological relationships rather than designing a sterile, single-crop garden.
* Composting and nutrient cycling: investigate composting suitable plant waste and returning safe, processed nutrients to growing areas.
* Food storage and distribution: consider how harvested food can be stored, shared and replenished to reduce waste.

Do not assume every proposed crop can grow in every location. Treat the crop list as a starting point for a site-specific growing plan.
3. The Hive principle: sustainable production
The Hive project's objective is to develop a system that can continually produce useful food while protecting the ecological processes on which production depends.
Investigate:

* What food can be produced per square metre per growing season?
* How much water and labour does each crop require?
* Which crops can be grown in rotation or together?
* How can pollinators be supported without compromising food safety?
* How can the system reuse rainwater and suitable organic waste?
* How can seasonal shortages, pests, disease and crop failure be managed?
* What food can realistically be produced for the number of residents?

Do not claim that the garden will produce more food than its residents consume until yields, land area, growing seasons, water needs and household demand have been calculated.
The Hive is a design hypothesis to be developed and tested, not an already validated self-sustaining agricultural system.
4. Bees and pollination
The project includes a proposed pollinator component called the Human Pollen / Pollen Project.
Its practical focus is to support pollination and food production by understanding the relationships between flowering plants, bees, other pollinators and crop yields.
Keep scientific claims evidence-based. In particular:

* Do not assume that wasps evolved from bees because of food shortages.
* Do not assume that all wasps are bees or that all bees are wasps.
* Bees and wasps are related groups within the order Hymenoptera, with distinct evolutionary histories and ecological roles.
* Design for beneficial pollinators while considering the needs of other native insects and wildlife.
* Avoid unnecessary pesticide use and provide safe separation between any managed bee colonies, residents, pathways and food-handling areas.

Do not add an active beehive to the architectural model unless it is explicitly approved. First represent a provisional pollinator habitat zone.

6. Required deliverables before detailed modelling
First produce a written Hive concept plan containing:

1. A simple system diagram showing how roof runoff, stored water, crops, pollinators, fish (if approved), compost and harvested food might relate.
2. A land-use schedule identifying proposed areas and their purposes.
3. A preliminary crop-and-season table.
4. A water-demand and rainwater-harvesting assessment.
5. A list of ecological, animal-welfare, food-safety and maintenance risks.
6. A clear list of assumptions, missing site data and decisions requiring approval.
7. A proposed layout that preserves the existing duplex footprint and distinguishes private or dwelling-associated areas from shared public facilities.

Only proceed to 3D geometry after the session confirms that SolidCraft tools are available and the protected-file checks pass. Use a separate proposal workspace and label all outputs:
`MMUCO ORION HIVE SYSTEM — CONCEPT PROPOSAL — NOT ISSUED`
7. Guiding principle
The Hive is intended to make the dwelling part of a productive ecosystem rather than a building that merely consumes external resources.
The goal is to explore how housing, food production, water management and ecological stewardship can work together within MMUCO Orion OS.
Build the concept carefully, measure what can be measured, distinguish verified facts from hypotheses, and preserve all existing approved housing work.
